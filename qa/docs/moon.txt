The Moon

The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite. It orbits at an average distance of about 384,400 kilometres, and its diameter of about 3,474 kilometres is roughly a quarter of Earth's. Gravity at its surface is about one sixth of the gravity on Earth.

Orbit and rotation

The Moon goes around Earth once every 27.3 days. Because it turns on its axis in the same time, the same side always faces Earth; this is called tidal locking. The full cycle of phases, from one new moon to the next, takes about 29.5 days. The Moon's gravity is the main cause of the ocean tides on Earth. Measurements with lasers show that the Moon is moving away from Earth by about 3.8 centimetres per year.

Origin and surface

The Moon formed about 4.5 billion years ago. The most widely accepted explanation is the giant-impact hypothesis: a body about the size of Mars struck the young Earth, and the Moon gathered from the debris. The dark plains seen from Earth are called maria. They are made of solidified basaltic lava. The brighter areas are older highlands covered in craters. The Moon has no substantial atmosphere, only a very thin exosphere, so there is no weather to wear the craters away. Water ice has been found in permanently shadowed craters near the poles.

Exploration

The Soviet probe Luna 2 became the first spacecraft to reach the surface of the Moon when it struck it in 1959. On 20 July 1969, Apollo 11 landed, and Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin became the first people to walk on the Moon, while Michael Collins stayed in orbit. Twelve astronauts walked on the Moon during six Apollo landings between 1969 and 1972. Apollo 17, in December 1972, was the last crewed mission to the Moon.
